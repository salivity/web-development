Serverless vs Provisioned Databases for Web Apps

Choosing between a serverless database and a provisioned database is a critical architectural decision in modern web development. While serverless databases offer automated scaling and pay-per-request pricing, provisioned databases provide dedicated resources with consistent performance and predictable costs. This article outlines the key technical and operational considerations—including traffic predictability, cost structures, latency tolerance, and maintenance overhead—to help developers select the optimal database model for their application.

Workload Predictability and Traffic Patterns

The nature of your application's traffic is the most significant factor in this decision.

Serverless databases excel when handling unpredictable, spiky, or infrequent traffic. Applications such as early-stage startups, seasonal e-commerce platforms, or internal tools with distinct business-hour usage benefit from serverless architectures because resources scale to zero during idle periods and scale up instantly during traffic surges.

Provisioned databases are better suited for steady, predictable workloads. When an application experiences a consistent baseline of incoming queries 24/7, provisioned instances eliminate the risk of scaling lag and offer a more cost-effective utilization rate per compute unit.

Cost Structure and Budget Control

Cost models differ fundamentally between the two approaches:

  • Serverless Databases: You pay strictly for consumption—typically measured in read/write capacity units, storage, and data transfer. This eliminates paying for idle capacity. However, sudden runaway queries or distributed denial-of-service (DDoS) traffic can lead to unexpected billing spikes without strict billing alerts and caps.
  • Provisioned Databases: You pay a fixed, recurring price for reserved compute (CPU, RAM) and allocated storage, regardless of how much is utilized. This provides complete budget predictability, though it risks paying for unused headroom during off-peak hours.

Latency and Cold Starts

Performance requirements often dictate database architecture:

  • Cold Starts: Serverless databases can suffer from initial latency spikes (cold starts) when scaling up from zero or provisioning new compute nodes under sudden load. If your application demands ultra-low, single-digit millisecond latency consistently, this behavior may be unacceptable.
  • Consistent Baseline: Provisioned databases keep compute and memory active continuously. Connection pools and caches remain warm, delivering deterministic query execution times and stable latency profiles.

Connection Management and Concurrency

Web architectures, particularly those built on serverless compute like AWS Lambda or Vercel, interact differently with each database type.

Traditional provisioned relational databases (e.g., standard PostgreSQL or MySQL) use persistent connections with strict upper limits. A surge in stateless serverless functions can quickly exhaust available database connections, requiring external connection poolers like PgBouncer or AWS RDS Proxy.

Serverless databases are purpose-built for high concurrency and stateless environments. They typically provide HTTP-based APIs or built-in connection routing capable of handling thousands of ephemeral connections without crashing the underlying instance.

Operational Overhead and Administration

  • Serverless: Offloads nearly all infrastructure management. The cloud provider handles patching, capacity planning, horizontal scaling, failover, and replication automatically. This allows small teams to focus entirely on application logic.
  • Provisioned: Requires active database administration. Teams must monitor disk space, CPU utilization, and memory pressure. Scaling vertically or horizontally often requires planning, manual intervention, or maintenance windows, although it provides deeper control over database configurations and fine-tuning.

Decision Summary

Opt for a serverless database if you are launching a new product with unknown demand, building on stateless compute layers, experiencing intermittent workloads, or lack dedicated database administrators.

Opt for a provisioned database if you have steady, high-volume throughput, require deterministic low latency without cold starts, need granular control over the database engine configuration, or require strictly predictable monthly infrastructure expenses.
